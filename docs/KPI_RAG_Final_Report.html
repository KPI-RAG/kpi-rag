Front matter

Abstract

Problem. Diagnosing a 5G fault means relating an anomalous pattern across dozens of KPIs to its physical cause and to the 3GPP specification that governs it. In network operations centres this is still done by hand and depends on the engineer’s experience. Classifiers name a fault without explaining it, and language models explain fluently but often cite the wrong standard.

Objective and method. KPI-RAG is an end-to-end pipeline that detects and explains faults and grounds each explanation in a standard that can be checked. On the TelecomTS dataset (32,000 windows of 18 KPI channels, 12.8 s each), a two-stage CatBoost cascade detects and classifies 11 fault types, and TreeSHAP identifies the KPIs behind each decision. The system then retrieves past incidents of the predicted type from a ChromaDB index built from training data only. An LLM (Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite) writes a structured explanation from a curated fault-to-3GPP alignment table (Release 19) and per-window evidence, and every citation it produces is checked automatically.

Results. On a session-based test split, the cascade reaches 88.7 % binary recall (precision 69.6 %, F1 0.780) and 66.3 % exact 11-class accuracy. In a 30-window ablation sampled by ground truth, the cited standard is correct for the true fault in 5/30 explanations with label and SHAP only, 7/30 with retrieved incidents, and 21/30 with alignment-table grounding (+47 points). When the fault is classified correctly, the grounded system cites the right standard in 19/19 cases. All nine misses come from detection or classification errors earlier in the pipeline.

Conclusion. With a curated alignment table and a citation validator, the cited standard is dependable once the fault has been identified. Most of the remaining error comes from fault detection, so that is where future work should go. Explanation-quality scoring (G-Eval) is in progress.

Keywords: 5G, root-cause analysis, retrieval-augmented generation, 3GPP, O-RAN, SHAP, CatBoost, explainable AI

Chapter 1

Introduction

Why fault explanations need standards that can be checked, and what the project set out to do

1.1 Background and motivation

5G New Radio and Open RAN split the base station into radio, distributed and central units connected by open interfaces. This flexibility multiplies the number of components and Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) an operations team must watch. When KPIs degrade, engineers compare raw measurements with the technical specifications by hand. The work is slow and its outcome depends on who does it [6], which lengthens the mean time to repair and raises operating cost.

Two families of tools address parts of this. Machine-learning classifiers detect and label faults accurately but give no reason an engineer can act on. Large language models produce fluent explanations, but without grounding they cite specifications that do not govern the fault (Section 5.3: 5 of 30 correct). A pipeline that joins the two and makes every citation checkable would help NOC engineers and researchers alike.

1.2 Problem statement

Given a window of 5G KPI measurements, the project addresses three connected sub-problems: (1) detect whether the window is anomalous; (2) classify the fault among eleven categories (ten synthetically injected, one real over-the-air jamming event); and (3) generate a root-cause explanation that cites the governing 3GPP specification and O-RAN component, in a form that can be verified automatically. To our knowledge no published system addresses all three in one evaluable pipeline on public data.

1.3 Objectives and research questions

The aim was to build and evaluate an end-to-end pipeline that detects and explains 5G network faults and grounds each explanation in a standard that can be checked. The proposal set five objectives:

  • O1 Detection and classification: a two-stage cascade on TelecomTS across 11 fault types, with binary F1 comparable to the strongest published result (Mantis, F1 = 0.800 [1]).
  • O2 Fault-to-standard alignment: a validated table linking the 10 synthetic fault types to specific 3GPP clauses and O-RAN components.
  • O3 Retrieval: retrieval of relevant past incidents driven by the SHAP attributions.
  • O4 Grounded explanation: at least 70 % of explanations pass the 3GPP citation check (pre-registered threshold).
  • O5 Evaluation: a joint protocol for detection and explanation, including a controlled ablation.

These lead to three research questions:

  • RQ1 How well does a two-stage CatBoost cascade detect and classify faults on a session-based split, compared with published baselines?
  • RQ2 Does alignment-table grounding produce more correct standards citations than ticket-only and ungrounded generation, and how much of the remaining error comes from the classifier?
  • RQ3 Are the explanations specific, actionable and causally sound (G-Eval)?

1.4 Scope

The system targets single-cell, KPI time-series fault diagnosis on the TelecomTS testbed: 10 synthetically injected fault types and one real jamming event, all from one indoor 5G testbed. Multi-cell correlation, cross-domain diagnosis across RAN, core and transport, and live production deployment are out of scope. Generalising to multi-vendor operator networks would require re-evaluation on operator data with naturally occurring fault labels.

1.5 Report organisation

Chapter 2 reviews related work and the gap addressed. Chapter 3 describes the methodology and evaluation design, Chapter 4 the architecture and implementation, and Chapter 5 the results. Chapter 6 interprets them against the objectives and states the limitations, and Chapter 7 concludes. The appendices hold the alignment table, per-window results, sample explanations, the evaluation rubric, configuration, user instructions and the test inventory.

Chapter 2

Background and Literature Review

Concepts, prior work, and the gap this project addresses

2.1 5G, O-RAN and network KPIs

The O-RAN Alliance [21] splits the gNB into the O-RU (RF front end, low PHY), the O-DU (high PHY, MAC, RLC, schedulers, buffers) and the O-CU (PDCP, SDAP, RRC), with the Near-RT RIC hosting control loops between 10 ms and 1 s for mobility, traffic steering and resource allocation. Localising a fault to one of these components is part of an actionable diagnosis.

TelecomTS [2] records 18 channels per window: 16 numeric KPIs (RSRP, DL_BLER, UL_BLER, DL_MCS, UL_MCS, UL_NPRB, UL_SNR, TX_Bytes, RX_Bytes, Estimated_UL_Buffer, PRBs_DL_Current, PRBs_UL_Current, PRB_Utilization_DL, PRB_Utilization_UL, UL_NumberOfPackets, DL_NumberOfPackets) and two protocol fields. Each anomalous window also carries a natural-language KPI narrative, a troubleshooting ticket and a Q&A trace. The eleven fault types are Antenna Failure, Buffer Overflow, Co-Channel Interference (Mild and Severe), Doppler Shift, Faulty Handover Algorithm, Faulty RF Filters, High Network Congestion (Gradual Buildup and Sudden Spike), Resource Allocation Bugs, and Jamming, which is the only real over-the-air anomaly.

2.2 Anomaly detection on TelecomTS

TelecomTS introduced the dataset and benchmarks for detection and root-cause classification [2]. Mantis, a lightweight calibrated foundation model for time-series classification, reports the strongest binary detection on it (F1 = 0.800). It outputs fault labels only, with no KPI attribution or standards reference [1]. NuTime shows that embeddings which keep the absolute scale of a time series perform better [3], and our feature design keeps raw-scale statistics for the same reason. Graph-attention models have shown KPI anomaly detection is viable at operator scale [4].

2.3 LLM-based root-cause analysis

A recent survey of LLMs for AIOps covers 183 papers and finds that standards-grounded explanation remains under-explored [6]. RCACopilot uses retrieval-augmented LLMs for cloud incidents in production (micro F1 0.766) but works on cloud metrics with no 3GPP citations [7]; Roy et al. study LLM agents for root-cause analysis and their groundedness [8]. In telecom, TN-AutoRCA builds an alarm-based RCA benchmark with an agentic framework [9], reasoning LLMs fine-tuned for 5G RCA outperform general models on drive-test data [10], and GALA augments LLM agents with graphs for RCA [12]. None of these links KPI-level detection to verifiable 3GPP clauses.

2.4 Retrieval and telecom knowledge

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions a language model on passages from an external store [13]. The passages are usually embedded with a bi-encoder such as Sentence-BERT [16] and searched with approximate nearest neighbours [22]. TeleQnA shows that RAG improves LLM accuracy on telecom-standards questions [5]. GSMA’s 3GPP Release 19 and O-RAN knowledge graphs [D2, D3] give structured access to specification content; the team used them to find candidate clauses and then confirmed each one against the primary specification text. Section 5.5 shows that RAG only helps when the corpus holds information the model does not already have.

2.5 Explainability and explanation evaluation

SHAP assigns each feature an additive Shapley contribution to a prediction [14]; TreeSHAP computes it exactly for tree ensembles such as CatBoost [15]. A SHAP value’s sign says whether a feature pushed toward the predicted class, not whether the KPI was high or low, so the system reports KPI direction separately. G-Eval scores generated text with an LLM judge following explicit rubric dimensions on Likert scales [11]; this project uses four telecom-specific dimensions (Appendix D).

2.6 Gap addressed

Gap

No published system connects KPI-level anomaly detection to a root-cause explanation that cites a specific, automatically verified 3GPP clause in one evaluable pipeline on public data, and no published work maps the TelecomTS fault taxonomy to 3GPP clauses and O-RAN components. KPI-RAG addresses both and measures the citation end to end against ground truth.

Chapter 3

Methodology

Data, models, retrieval, grounding and evaluation, with the reason for each choice

3.1 Approach overview

The pipeline has three stages. In detection, a binary model flags anomalous windows, an 11-class model names the fault, and TreeSHAP attributes the decision to individual KPIs. In grounding, the system retrieves past incidents of the predicted fault, takes the governing standard and O-RAN component from that fault’s row of the alignment table, and builds a per-window block summarising what drove the decision. In the explanation stage an LLM writes a structured JSON explanation, and its citation is validated against the table. Figure 1 (Chapter 4) shows the architecture.

The proposal planned a window-level split, under which adjacent train and test windows overlap by 75 %. Phase 2 adopted a session-based split instead, which removes that overlap and gives a stricter estimate. Only the 935 anomalous training windows are ever indexed for retrieval, so no test window can retrieve itself; the build fails if the split file is missing or the dataset order does not match the ground truth.

3.3 Features and detection cascade

The Layer 2 models were built by the ML team (Farida El Ghabary, Raneem Alnaghy). Model 1 uses 64 base features (mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum of the 16 numeric KPIs). Model 2 uses 496: the 64 base statistics; 256 temporal-patch statistics (8 patches × 16 KPIs × mean and standard deviation); 80 spectral features (four FFT band energies plus total energy per KPI); 84 temporal-shape features (shift, log-std ratio, trend, convexity, S-shape); and 10 onset, cross-KPI correlation, difference and radio-share features.

CatBoost [15] was chosen over the proposal’s Random Forest after comparison by the ML team; it keeps exact TreeSHAP support. Models were selected on internal validation criteria; because the same test sessions were also consulted during model development, the detection figures are best read as a test-split estimate. An isotonic calibrator was evaluated and rejected, so the reported confidence is uncalibrated: for an alarm it is Model 2’s probability of the predicted fault type.

3.4 SHAP attribution

For each alarm, TreeSHAP values are computed for the predicted class and the top three single-KPI features kept. Each entry records the magnitude |SHAP|, its effect (toward or away from the predicted class) and whether the window’s value lies above or below the normal-traffic mean (feature_vs_normal, from training normals). Prompts show all three, for example “RSRP: below normal (|SHAP| = 0.42, toward predicted class)”, so the model never has to infer direction or effect from a sign. Of the 1,146 top-3 entries, 97 point away from the predicted class.

3.5 Retrieval of past incidents

The corpus is the 935 anomalous training windows, one document per window: its own KPI narrative. Documents are embedded with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [16, 17] in a cosine-space ChromaDB index [18]. The query carries the window’s KPI evidence (top SHAP channels, their directions, uplink/downlink means) and leaves out the fault label. Results are filtered to incidents of the predicted fault type, and the top three are shown to the LLM.

Why this design

Section 5.5 gives the measurements behind it. The dataset’s tickets are one template per fault class, so retrieving on tickets only repeated the label. Text evidence on its own identifies the true class only modestly above chance. The classifier therefore supplies the class as a filter, and retrieval ranks comparable incidents within that class.

3.6 Standards grounding and RCA evidence

The 3GPP alignment table v1.2 (Rodina Khallaf) maps each of the 10 synthetic fault types to Release 19 clauses and an O-RAN component (Appendix A); all ten rows are confirmed against primary specification text. Jamming is marked not-applicable because no conformance clause governs an external over-the-air attack. Every prompt takes its standards from this table and nowhere else. A citation counts as valid for a fault if it is a well-formed TS/TR number listed in that fault’s row; a fault without a row can never have a valid citation.

The RCA evidence file holds 289 records, one per non-Jamming alarm on the test split. For a window whose record matches its predicted fault, a compact block of 537 to 659 characters is injected into the grounded prompt: the top-3 SHAP features with magnitude, direction and effect, the observed means of the KPIs behind them, and the fault’s causal mechanism. The record never contains the window’s true label.

3.7 Explanation generation and citation check

Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite [19] runs at temperature 0.1 with native JSON output, returning five fields: root cause, 3GPP reference, O-RAN component, recommended action and reasoning trace. The reference is normalised to TS/TR XX.XXX and validated in two steps: first the format, then whether it appears in the fault’s table row. Calls are spaced at least 2 s apart. On a rate-limit response the client waits for the server’s Retry-After, up to 60 s, while configuration and authentication errors stop the run at once. Malformed replies are retried, and after three failed attempts the system returns a template explanation that is labelled with its cause and always counted as invalid. Groq and Ollama are supported alternatives.

3.8 Evaluation design

  • Track A (detection and classification). Recall, precision, F1 and exact and family-level accuracy on the 6,312 test windows, recomputed from the Layer 2 predictions and ground truth and checked against the ML team’s own validation file.
  • Track C (citation ablation). Three test windows per true fault type (10 types, seed 42), each run through the full system under three conditions that share one instruction block and differ only in context: C1 gets the label and SHAP; C2 adds three past incidents; C3 adds the alignment row and RCA evidence. Each citation is scored against the true fault’s row, so a misclassification that injects the wrong row counts as wrong, and a detector miss produces no explanation and also counts as incorrect. Two rules that need no LLM are scored the same way: always citing the single best standard, and citing the predicted fault’s table row.
  • Track B (explanation quality). G-Eval [11] with four dimensions scored 1 to 5 (citation validity, fault specificity, actionability, causal soundness; Appendix D) on every Track C explanation.
  • Retrieval diagnostics. Precision@1 and @5 of the retrieved incidents’ fault type against the true class, for alternative document and query designs.

With n = 30 per condition drawn from a small number of recording sessions, Wilson 95 % intervals and exact McNemar tests describe the size of the effects; with this sample they should not be read as significance tests.

3.9 Tools

Python 3.11 managed with uv; CatBoost and SHAP (Layer 2, offline); sentence-transformers and ChromaDB 1.5 (retrieval); google-genai, groq and openai clients (LLM); pydantic schemas; Streamlit 1.61 (dashboard); pytest (testing). All software is open source; the only paid-tier service is optional.

4.2 Components

Data pipeline: src/data_loader.py, scripts/build_index.py

Reads the JSONL files in sorted order and flattens anomalous records into tickets. Because raw records carry no identifier, a ticket’s id is its position; verify_ticket_order() checks every ticket’s label against the ground truth before indexing, and the build refuses to clear the index when no tickets were loaded.

Layer 2 interface: src/ml_model.py

The models are trained offline by the ML pipeline and consumed here as a JSON of predictions, SHAP entries and window statistics; ml_model.py loads the CatBoost files for verification and future online inference.

Retrieval: src/kg_indexer.py, src/rag_query.py

These modules embed each training window’s narrative, build the label-free evidence query, apply the predicted-type metadata filter and flag results below a similarity of 0.35.

Explanation: src/llm_explainer.py, src/rca_loader.py

Builds the prompt from shared parts (header with label, confidence and SHAP; optional incidents, evidence and standards blocks; one common instruction block), calls the LLM with retries, parses and normalises the JSON, and validates the citation. The RCA loader formats the evidence block of Section 3.6.

Evaluation: scripts/run_eval_track_a.py, run_eval_track_c.py, experiment_retrieval_design.py

The Track A script recomputes all detection and classification metrics. The Track C script samples by ground truth, runs the three conditions, scores them together with the no-LLM rules and writes a summary; it will not overwrite an earlier run. The retrieval experiment reproduces the design comparison in Section 5.5.

4.3 Dashboard

The Streamlit dashboard (dashboard/app.py) presents six panels:

  1. Detection: predicted fault and Model 2 probability;
  2. SHAP: top contributing KPIs;
  3. KPI window statistics;
  4. Explanation: root cause, action, reasoning, and a ✅/❌ citation badge (with the cause if a template was used);
  5. Sources: retrieved past incidents with similarity, flagged when weakly similar;
  6. RCA evidence: standard and O-RAN component from the alignment table, model attribution and KPI coverage.

The input can be a built-in example, an uploaded ClassifierOutput JSON, or one of the 382 test windows the detector flagged, filtered by fault type. These are all test windows, so none of them has its own ticket in the index. Uploads are keyed by content, so a new file never shows an old explanation, and the dashboard asks for the API key of whichever LLM backend is configured.

The test suite has 209 tests in 29 modules (21 unit, 8 integration). Without the table, the LLM’s citations are unreliable, and both C1 and C2 score below a fixed answer. C1 mostly cites TS 38.104 (11), TS 38.331 (8) and TS 38.214 (4), plus one LTE specification (TS 36.104). Retrieved incidents change little: C2 improves 2 windows over C1 and worsens none.

5.4 Track B: explanation quality (G-Eval)

In progress

Rodina Khallaf is running G-Eval on the 72 explanations from Track C v3 (track_c_v3/track_c_scores_template.jsonl). Each explanation is tagged with its true fault and judged against that fault’s alignment row on the four dimensions in Appendix D. An earlier scoring pass used explanations sampled by predicted label, which included normal and misclassified windows, so its scores are not reported. The scoring tool rejects rows without scores, which prevents an incomplete run from being averaged by mistake.

5.5 Retrieval quality

The dataset’s ticket text has 11 distinct values, one template per class, and the root-cause answer in each Q&A is the class name. Retrieving on tickets therefore cannot add anything the label does not already say. The deployed design takes the class from the classifier as a filter and ranks incidents by KPI evidence. Averaging the embeddings of all paragraphs in a narrative did much worse, because the radio-layer evidence sits in the first paragraph. Over the 382 alarms in deployment, the similarity of the top retrieved incident ranges from 0.59 to 0.75 (median 0.72), and the low-confidence flag never fired.

5.6 Software testing

206 of 209 tests pass; the 3 skipped tests need a Groq API key. The suite also reproduces the ML team’s validation counts from the committed predictions (Appendix G lists the modules).

Chapter 6

Discussion

What the results mean, how they compare with the objectives, and their limits

6.1 Interpretation of results

RQ1. The cascade detects 88.7 % of test faults with 69.6 % precision (F1 0.780, against the strongest published 0.800) and classifies 66.3 % exactly. Performance varies by class. Jamming, Doppler and Resource Allocation Bugs are close to perfect, congestion is mostly confused with Buffer Overflow, and the detector misses every Sudden Spike window.

RQ2. Yes. With alignment-table grounding the citation is correct whenever the fault is classified correctly (19/19), and end-to-end correctness rises from 7/30 to 21/30. C3 matches the copy-row rule exactly, which confirms that the curated table and the validator make the citation dependable. The LLM reliably uses the standard it is given; left to choose on its own, it does worse than a fixed answer. Every remaining error comes from Layer 2, so better detection will directly give better explanations.

RQ3 awaits the Track B scoring (Section 5.4). That track measures what the LLM adds beyond the citation: how specific, actionable and causally sound the explanation is.

6.3 Lessons from building the system

  1. Check what the corpus contains before building retrieval on it. The tickets were class templates, so retrieval with the label in the query only repeated the label. Measuring this changed the design.
  2. Sample evaluations by ground truth. An early ablation sampled by predicted label, which mixed normal and misclassified windows into the results. Sampling by true fault separates the grounding effect from classifier errors.
  3. Keep ablation conditions identical except for the variable under test. All three conditions share one instruction block, so the difference between C2 and C3 comes from the context alone.
  4. Baselines without an LLM make a citation metric interpretable. Here they show that the gain comes from the table, which is how the system was designed to work.
  5. Keep one source for standards. When standards came from both the alignment table and the RCA records, updating the table left the prompt with two conflicting references. The table is now the only source.
  6. Show what an attribution means. Layer 2 stores |SHAP|, and displaying it as a signed value hid the fact that some top features pushed against the predicted class.
  7. Fail closed. A missing split file, an empty raw-data folder or an invalid API key now stops the pipeline, where before it could produce plausible but wrong output.

6.4 Practical implications

For a network operations centre, a curated fault-to-standard table and a validator make citations dependable once the fault is identified, whereas an ungrounded LLM often cites the wrong specification. The next improvements should come from detection (Sudden Spike, congestion) and from extending the table. The LLM contributes the explanation it writes around the standard. Because the dashboard shows the citation check, the retrieved precedents and the evidence behind each explanation, an engineer can judge how far to trust it.

6.5 Limitations

  • The citation metric is specification-level, and in C3 the standard is in the prompt. Clause numbers are not checked. C3 measures how reliably the grounded system cites the right standard; what the LLM knows unaided is what C1 measures.
  • Small, clustered sample. n = 30 per condition, 3 per class, from a few recording sessions; intervals and p-values are descriptive.
  • Test-split estimate. Detection figures come from the test sessions, which were also consulted during model development. Precision also depends on which recording sessions fall in the test split.
  • Jamming weight. Without the Jamming class, exact accuracy is 51.5 %.
  • Undetected fault types. Sudden Spike congestion is never detected and gradual congestion is mostly mislabelled, which caps end-to-end correctness for those faults whatever the LLM does.
  • Grounding amplifies classifier errors. A misclassified window receives confident-looking evidence for the wrong class.
  • Explanation quality is not yet scored. G-Eval is in progress, and a judge from the same model family as the generator should be checked against human ratings.
  • Single testbed and model. One indoor testbed, ten synthetic fault types, one LLM and one seed on a free-tier quota.
  • No resolution text in the corpus. Retrieval contributes comparable KPI patterns, not past fixes.
Chapter 7

Conclusion and Future Work

Achievements, contributions and next steps

7.1 Main achievements

  1. An end-to-end pipeline from KPI windows to standards-cited explanations, with a six-panel dashboard deployed on Streamlit Community Cloud.
  2. A session-split detection cascade with 88.7 % recall (F1 0.780) and 66.3 % exact accuracy over 11 fault types.
  3. Grounded explanations whose citation is correct for the true fault in 21 of 30 sampled faults and in 19 of 19 correctly classified ones, which meets the pre-registered 70 % threshold.
  4. A reproducible evaluation, with 209 automated tests, committed result files and scripts that regenerate every reported number.

7.2 Contributions

  • Artifact: the first Release 19 fault-to-3GPP / O-RAN alignment table for the TelecomTS taxonomy.
  • Method: a three-condition citation ablation sampled and scored by ground truth, with no-LLM baselines that show what the metric can and cannot measure.
  • Empirical finding: on TelecomTS, retrieval over the dataset’s tickets cannot replace the classifier. The grounding comes from the curated standards, and detection sets the upper bound on end-to-end quality.

7.3 Future work

  • Complete G-Eval on the 72 explanations and spot-check how well the judge agrees with human ratings.
  • Improve detection of Sudden Spike and gradual congestion, and re-estimate Track A on sessions untouched by model development.
  • Validate citations at clause level, and add a test the prompt cannot answer (e.g. hold the table out for some faults).
  • Scale Track C to more windows, sessions, seeds and a second LLM.
  • Run Layer 2 inference inside the repository (named features, a feature builder), and regenerate the RCA evidence from table v1.2.
  • Extend to multi-cell and cross-domain diagnosis, and evaluate on operator data with naturally occurring faults.

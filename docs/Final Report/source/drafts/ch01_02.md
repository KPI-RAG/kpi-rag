## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Background and motivation

Fifth-generation (5G) radio access networks (RANs) built on the O-RAN (Open Radio Access Network) Alliance architecture split the base station into radio units (O-RU), distributed units (O-DU) and centralised units (O-CU) connected by open interfaces. A near-real-time RAN Intelligent Controller (Near-RT RIC) hosts control loops [@oran2022arch]. The split multiplies the components and key performance indicators (KPIs) that an operations team has to watch, and the behaviour of each part is governed by the specifications of the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP). KPIs can turn anomalous through co-channel interference, Doppler shift, buffer overflow or radio-frequency (RF) jamming, for instance. A network operations centre (NOC) engineer then needs to know, beyond the fault label, why the fault occurred, which specification governs the observed behaviour, which component to act on and what corrective action is appropriate.

This diagnosis is largely manual. Engineers correlate raw KPI measurements with the specifications by hand, which is slow, inconsistent and hard to scale with fault volume [@zhang2025aiops]. Each incident depends on individual expertise, which lengthens the mean time to repair.

Machine-learning (ML) classifiers trained on KPI time series detect and name faults, but most fault-detection studies stop at the label. Large language models (LLMs) write fluent explanations, but without grounding they often cite a standard that does not apply to the fault. In the final citation ablation of this project, the system with only the predicted label and the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) evidence in the prompt cited the correct specification for 5 of 30 sampled faults (Section 5.7). KPI-RAG, the system developed by Group 15, detects and classifies faults from KPI windows and passes each alarm to a retrieval-augmented explanation layer that takes the cited standard from a curated alignment table (matrix v1.2) and validates the citation automatically.

### 1.2 Problem statement

Given a window of 5G KPI measurements, the project addresses three connected sub-problems:

1. **Detection.** Decide whether the window shows anomalous behaviour.
2. **Classification.** Identify the fault type among eleven categories: ten fault types injected into normal recordings and one real over-the-air jamming event.
3. **Explanation.** Produce an explanation that names a physical root cause tied to the window's KPI values, cites the 3GPP specification and the O-RAN component involved, recommends an action, and can be verified automatically.

The sub-problems are coupled: a missed fault receives no explanation, and a misclassified fault receives evidence and a standard that belong to another fault. The evaluation therefore has to measure how often the citation is right, and how much of the remaining error belongs to the explanation layer and how much to the classifier in front of it. To the best of our knowledge, no published system addresses all three sub-problems in a single evaluable pipeline on public benchmark data.

### 1.3 Objectives and research questions

The primary objective is to design, implement and evaluate an end-to-end pipeline that detects and explains 5G network faults with verifiable standards grounding. The five objectives set in the project proposal were kept throughout:

- **O1 Anomaly detection and fault classification.** Train a scale-aware two-stage pipeline on TelecomTS across 11 fault types from 18 KPI channels, quantifying the contribution of absolute-scale feature preservation; Model 1 is measured by binary detection F1 and Model 2 by macro-F1 across all 11 classes.
- **O2 Fault-to-standard alignment.** Construct and validate an alignment table linking the 10 injected fault types to specific 3GPP clauses and O-RAN components (Jamming has no corresponding clause), cross-referenced against the GSMA knowledge graphs and confirmed against the primary specification text.
- **O3 Standards retrieval.** Build a retrieval pipeline that uses SHAP attributions as the structured query; the proposal's criterion was acceptable precision@k on held-out alignment-table queries.
- **O4 Grounded explanation.** Generate plain-language reports in which every claim is grounded in a retrieved clause or ticket, with at least 70 % passing the regex-validated 3GPP clause check. The final evaluation applies this check to the specification number; clause numbers are not checked (Section 3.11).
- **O5 Evaluation.** Define a protocol that evaluates detection and explanation faithfulness jointly, which no existing benchmark does.

Three research questions follow:

- **RQ1** Does scale-preserving feature engineering improve detection F1 and classification macro-F1 over normalised-feature and statistics-only baselines?
- **RQ2** Does alignment-table grounding produce measurably higher citation faithfulness than ticket-only and ungrounded LLM generation?
- **RQ3** Which feature configuration offers the best performance-to-cost trade-off?

Four evaluation tracks assess the objectives. Track A covers detection and classification, and Track B scores explanation quality with an LLM judge (G-Eval). Track C is the citation ablation, and Track D tests retrieval-augmented question answering on the TeleQnA benchmark to map the limits of the system's knowledge (Section 3.11).

### 1.4 Scope

The project covers single-cell, single-modality fault diagnosis from KPI time series on the public TelecomTS dataset [@feng2025telecomts; @feng2026telecomtshf]. Multi-cell correlation, cross-domain root-cause analysis spanning the RAN, core and transport networks, and live production deployment are out of scope. The deployed dashboard runs retrieval and the LLM explanation live on stored or uploaded classifier outputs; the classifier does not run inside the application.

**Scope of validity.** All results come from a single indoor 5G testbed with ten injected fault types and one controlled real anomaly (jamming). The data were recorded in 33 sessions, and only 10 of the 28 training sessions contain faults, two of them with Jamming; this is the dominant limit on both models. The citation results rest on 30 sampled windows, one LLM (Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite [@gemini]) and one seed, and they check the cited specification but not its clause. Generalisation to multi-vendor, multi-cell and multi-operator networks is out of scope, and production use would need re-evaluation on operator data with naturally occurring fault labels (Section 6.5).

### 1.5 Contributions

A first prototype of every layer was completed in September 2026; the team then measured it, audited it against its own artefacts and corrected the defects it found. The final system of October 2026 makes six contributions:

- **An end-to-end system.** A two-stage CatBoost cascade [@prokhorenkova2018catboost] with SHAP attribution feeds a retrieval-augmented explanation layer whose 3GPP citation is checked automatically. A public six-panel Streamlit dashboard [@streamlit] presents each step, and an automated test suite checks the code (Ahmed Al Shobaki built the retrieval and explanation layer, the dashboard, the test suite and the deployment).
- **A standards alignment table.** It maps the TelecomTS fault categories to 3GPP Release 19 clauses and O-RAN components and defines which citations the validator accepts (Rodina Khallaf).
- **Evaluation methods.** Model decisions were made by session-grouped cross-validation under a frozen protocol, on recording sessions reconstructed by Raneem Alnaghy. The citation ablation samples and scores windows by their true fault, which separates the effect of grounding from upstream errors (Ahmed Al Shobaki). An LLM judge scores explanation quality, and a TeleQnA test maps the knowledge scope of the retrieval setup (Rodina Khallaf).
- **A corrected detector and classifier.** Two defects in the first detector were fixed, and synthetic training data and trend features were added to the classifier (the ML team: Farida El Ghabary, Raneem Alnaghy).
- **Grounded citations.** In the final citation ablation, the explanation cited the right standard for every sampled window that the classifier labelled correctly, and every remaining error started in detection or classification.
- **Findings about the data and the retrieval layer.** The dataset's troubleshooting tickets are one template per fault class, so the deployed retrieval ranks per-window KPI narratives instead. Outside the fault-diagnosis domain, irrelevant retrieved context lowers accuracy, and a confidence gate on retrieval removes almost all of that loss.

## 2. Background and Literature Review

### 2.1 5G, O-RAN and network KPIs

The O-RAN Alliance architecture [@oran2022arch] divides the 5G base station (gNB) into three units. The O-RU holds the RF front end and the lower physical layer. The O-DU holds the upper physical layer, medium access control (MAC), radio link control (RLC), the schedulers and the buffers. The O-CU holds the packet data convergence protocol (PDCP), the service data adaptation protocol (SDAP) and radio resource control (RRC). The Near-RT RIC hosts control loops for mobility, traffic steering and resource allocation. A useful diagnosis names the component to act on as well as the fault, so every explanation that KPI-RAG produces has an O-RAN component field.

The behaviour that a fault disturbs is defined in 3GPP documents. Technical Specifications (TS) are normative, while Technical Reports (TR) are informative study documents; the citation validator accepts both prefixes because the Doppler Shift row of the alignment table (matrix v1.2) cites a TR. The final alignment table draws on six Release 19 documents. Three cover the base station and the MAC layer: base-station conformance testing, TS 38.141-1 [@ts38141]; base-station radio transmission and reception, TS 38.104 [@ts38104]; and the MAC protocol, TS 38.321 [@ts38321]. The other three are the RRC protocol, TS 38.331 [@ts38331]; 5G performance measurements, TS 28.552 [@ts28552]; and the channel model for 0.5 to 100 GHz, TR 38.901 [@tr38901].

The KPIs studied here are those recorded by TelecomTS: 18 channels spanning the physical, MAC and network layers, of which 16 are numerical KPIs and 2 are categorical protocol fields (uplink and downlink protocol). The numerical KPIs are reference signal received power (RSRP), UL_SNR, UL_MCS, DL_MCS, UL_BLER, DL_BLER, UL_NPRB, PRBs_UL_Current, PRBs_DL_Current, PRB_Utilization_UL, PRB_Utilization_DL, TX_Bytes, RX_Bytes, UL_NumberOfPackets, DL_NumberOfPackets and Estimated_UL_Buffer. Absolute levels carry diagnostic information: an RSRP of −90 dBm means something different from one of −70 dBm. This dependence on absolute level is the basis of the scale-preserving feature design that RQ1 tests.

### 2.2 Anomaly detection and fault classification on TelecomTS

TelecomTS [@feng2025telecomts; @feng2026telecomtshf] is a multi-modal observability dataset that combines time series and language, recorded on a real 5G lab network. It contains 32,000 windows of 128 samples at 100 ms (12.8 s), of which 1,235 (3.9 %) are anomalous. The anomalies fall into 11 categories: one real over-the-air jamming event, captured with a USRP X310 jammer, and ten fault types injected into normal recordings following KPI-effect mappings validated against the telecom engineering literature. Adjacent windows overlap by 75 % (a stride of 32 steps over 128). Each anomalous window comes with three texts: a KPI narrative written for that window, a troubleshooting ticket and a question-and-answer trace. Section 3.10 shows that in the training data the tickets take only 11 distinct values, one per fault class, so the narrative is the only text specific to the window.

The TelecomTS paper benchmarks published models on its own data [@feng2025telecomts]. Mantis, a lightweight foundation model for time-series classification introduced by Feofanov et al. [@feofanov2025mantis], reaches an F1 score of 0.800 there for binary anomaly detection, and Toto reaches an accuracy of 0.848 on the root-cause (fault-type) task [@feng2025telecomts]. Mantis classifies windows but provides no KPI-level attribution and no standards-grounded explanation. Both figures come from a random split of overlapping windows, so neither is directly comparable with the session-level results of this project (Section 5.5). Because adjacent windows overlap, a split by individual window leaks near-duplicates into the test set: with a window-level split, 31.8 % of the first prototype's test windows had a training window with a cosine similarity of at least 0.999 (Section 3.3).

Lin et al. propose NuTime, a numerically multi-scaled embedding for large-scale time-series pretraining [@lin2024nutime]. KPI-RAG adopts its general principle of capturing local patch-level statistics that keep their absolute magnitude, with a different architecture and task. Malacarne et al. apply scalable context-aware graph attention to unsupervised anomaly detection in large-scale mobile networks [@malacarne2026graph], which demonstrates that machine-learning KPI monitoring can be applied in a production telecom environment.

The models compared in this project come from two families. Tree ensembles work on features computed from each window: CatBoost [@prokhorenkova2018catboost], LightGBM [@ke2017lightgbm], XGBoost [@chen2016xgboost], Random Forest [@breiman2001rf] and ExtraTrees [@geurts2006extratrees], with Isolation Forest [@liu2008iforest] as the unsupervised detection baseline of the first prototype. Dedicated time-series classifiers were compared with them for the fault classifier: QUANT [@dempster2024quant], MiniRocket [@dempster2021minirocket], Hydra [@dempster2023hydra] and the catch22 feature set [@lubba2019catch22] combined with CatBoost (Sections 5.1 and 5.3). This project found that, on TelecomTS, CatBoost and QUANT fail on different faults, because QUANT relies on quantile and shape features and CatBoost on KPI levels (Section 5.3).

These studies show that automated anomaly detection is feasible on benchmark data and in operational networks, but their aim is detection or classification. None of them connects the observed KPI behaviour to a network component, a technical standard or an operational root cause.

### 2.3 Retrieval-augmented generation and telecom knowledge

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) conditions a language model on passages fetched from an external store [@lewis2020rag]. The passages are usually embedded with a bi-encoder such as Sentence-BERT [@reimers2019sbert]. KPI-RAG uses `all-MiniLM-L6-v2`, a distilled MiniLM model [@wang2020minilm] with about 22 million parameters, 384-dimensional embeddings and a 256-token input window, and stores the vectors in ChromaDB [@chroma], which searches hierarchical navigable small world (HNSW) graphs for approximate nearest neighbours [@malkov2020hnsw].

In telecom knowledge tasks, retrieval can supply information that the model parameters do not hold reliably. Maatouk et al. introduce TeleQnA [@maatouk2025teleqna], a multiple-choice benchmark of telecommunications questions derived from 3GPP standards documentation, with categories that include standards specifications, standards overview, lexicon, research overview and research publications. TeleQnA shows that retrieval improves LLM accuracy on telecom-standards questions. Retrieval helps only when the store holds something the model does not already have, and testing this assumption on TelecomTS changed the retrieval design of this project (Section 5.6). Irrelevant context can also lower accuracy: Shi et al. show that LLMs are easily distracted by it [@shi2023distracted], and Track D uses this effect to explain its results.

The GSMA telecom-kg-rel19 knowledge graph covers 3GPP Release 19 [@yang2026kgrel19], and the GSMA O-RAN specification knowledge graph covers the O-RAN documents [@gsma2026orankg]. The first prototype searched both graphs by keyword to find candidate clauses and O-RAN components for the alignment table, and confirmed each final citation against the primary specification text (Section 3.9).

### 2.4 LLM-based root-cause analysis

Zhang et al. survey AIOps (artificial intelligence for information technology operations) in the era of LLMs [@zhang2025aiops]. Across 183 papers they identify domain grounding, reliable reasoning and the evaluation of generated outputs as open challenges for LLM-based operational diagnosis, and they find standards-grounded explanation under-explored.

Several systems apply LLMs to root-cause analysis directly. RCACopilot (Chen et al.) uses retrieval-augmented LLMs for the root-cause analysis of cloud incidents in production, combining LLM reasoning with operational information [@chen2024rcacopilot]. Roy et al. study LLM-based agents with tools for root-cause analysis and stress the need to evaluate the quality and groundedness of their answers [@roy2024agents]. GALA (Tian et al.) adds graphs to LLM agent workflows, so that structured relationships inform the reasoning [@tian2025gala]; it is not designed around telecom KPI windows or clause-level 3GPP and O-RAN grounding. In telecommunications, Wu et al. introduce TN-AutoRCA, a benchmark and agentic framework for alarm-based root-cause analysis [@wu2025tnautorca], and Sana et al. show that reasoning language models fine-tuned for 5G root-cause analysis outperform general models on drive-test data [@sana2025reasoning]. {tab:related} compares the inputs and outputs of these systems.

None of these systems works on KPI windows, and none produces a 3GPP citation checked automatically against the fault. An LLM asked for a standard number produces one that looks plausible whether or not it applies. Putting the authoritative fact in the prompt and checking the output against a known set of valid answers reduce this risk, and KPI-RAG uses both. The fault's row of the alignment table goes into the prompt, and a validator accepts the citation only if it is a well-formed TS or TR number listed in that row. Asking the model for JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) with fixed fields makes the check deterministic.

### 2.5 Explainability and explanation evaluation

SHAP [@lundberg2017shap] gives each feature an additive contribution to a prediction, and its TreeExplainer computes these contributions for tree ensembles without a graphics processor. The sign of a SHAP value says whether the feature pushed the prediction towards or away from the predicted class. Whether the KPI was above or below its normal level is a separate fact, so an explanation built on SHAP has to state both. In KPI-RAG the top three SHAP features of each alarm link the classifier to the explanation layer: they form the retrieval query and part of the evidence in the prompt.

Explanations also need their own evaluation, because an explanation can be plausible and still lack relevance, consistency or grounding, and classification metrics do not capture this. Liu et al. introduce G-Eval [@liu2023geval], which uses an LLM to score generated text against predefined criteria and reports improved alignment with human judgements. Generic text-quality criteria do not cover the needs of standards-grounded diagnosis, so Track B adapts G-Eval to four project-defined dimensions: citation validity, fault specificity, actionability and causal soundness (Section 3.11). A judge's rating of a citation and the automatic citation check of Track C are different measurements.

### 2.6 Research gap

The reviewed work leaves three gaps. First, time-series classifiers such as Mantis and the detection studies on TelecomTS and mobile networks stop at detection or classification and do not connect the KPI pattern to a standards-grounded explanation [@feofanov2025mantis; @feng2025telecomts; @malacarne2026graph]. The TelecomTS paper's own benchmark results also come from a random split of overlapping windows, so they do not show how a detector performs on recording sessions it has not seen. Second, telecom knowledge benchmarks such as TeleQnA evaluate question answering, not explanation from observed KPI anomalies [@maatouk2025teleqna], and the 3GPP and O-RAN knowledge graphs provide structured content without a pipeline that starts from a KPI window [@yang2026kgrel19; @gsma2026orankg]. Third, LLM-based root-cause analysis systems work on other inputs and in other domains [@chen2024rcacopilot; @roy2024agents; @tian2025gala; @wu2025tnautorca; @sana2025reasoning]. They explain incidents but do not produce a standards citation that is verified automatically, and they are not evaluated in a way that separates the explanation step from upstream detection errors.

To the best of our knowledge, the reviewed literature has not demonstrated a single evaluable pipeline that connects KPI-level anomaly detection on a public telecom benchmark with a standards-grounded root-cause explanation whose citation is checked automatically. Nor does it establish, within one evaluation framework, an explicit mapping between TelecomTS fault categories, observed KPI behaviour, 3GPP references and O-RAN components. KPI-RAG addresses these gaps with a detector and classifier evaluated on held-out recording sessions, a standards alignment table and a grounded explanation layer with automatic citation validation. Its evaluation scores each citation against the true fault and traces every remaining error to the stage where it began. Chapter 3 describes the methods used to build and evaluate each part.
